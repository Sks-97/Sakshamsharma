The angle

Norms are the product, not definitions

Everybody explains what a liquidation preference is. Almost nobody says what’s normal — which is the only thing that tells you whether you’re being had.

That’s the whole differentiation and it follows from where you actually sit. A lawyer will define an ISO more accurately than you ever will. A lawyer will not tell a 28-year-old brand lead that four years with a one-year cliff is the default, that ninety days is the usual exercise window, or that nobody ever asks about the preference stack and the asking is free. Calibration is the product. You have it because you’ve been in the rooms; they don’t because nobody has ever shown them a second example.

So every idea below carries two parts — the hook, and the usual. If an idea has no norm attached, it’s a definition and it belongs to somebody else.

The wedge inside the audience: brand, design, content and partnerships people inside tech and startup companies. They hold the same instruments as the engineers, and every piece of equity content on the internet is written for someone who reads a term sheet for fun.

Shapes

Eight recurring formats

Pick the shape before the topic. A format the audience recognises is what turns sixty-nine ideas into a series rather than sixty-nine posts — and the first one below is the franchise.

The usual / not the usual

Two columns. What's standard, then the version you were handed. The whole franchise in one shape — it turns a definition into a diagnosis, and the viewer checks their own paperwork while watching.

“Four years, one year cliff. That's the usual. Six years with a two-year cliff is not a company, it's a holding pen.”

The sentence you say back

One moment, one line, delivered slowly enough to be written down. The highest-utility format you can make and the most re-watched.

“When they say we'll make it right at the next round — 'happy to wait, can we put the number and the date in the offer?'”

What they said / what it means

Translation. Recruiter language, brand-side language, investor language, decoded. Works because these people all speak in the same eight euphemisms.

“'We don't have budget' has five meanings and only one of them is no.”

The number

One benchmark, held on screen, no build-up. Shortest format, travels furthest, needs the most verification before you say it out loud.

“Ninety days. That's how long you have to find the money after you leave.”

Red flag / just unusual

Calibration. Half of what scares people is standard and half of what looks standard should scare them. Being the person who can tell them apart is the whole authority claim.

“Participating preferred isn't illegal. It's just expensive, and it's expensive to you specifically.”

Ask this one thing

A single question, plus what the answer tells you and what their face tells you. Self-contained, endlessly repeatable, works at any point in the series.

“Ask what the total preference is ahead of you. They will tell you. Nobody ever asks.”

The arithmetic, out loud

Green screen, numbers appearing as you say them. Reserved for the four or five concepts that only land when someone watches the figure move.

“One percent, three rounds, twenty percent each. Say it with me.”

I was on the other side of this

Your unfair advantage, used sparingly. Not a story format — a credential format. One sentence of it buys you the rest of the video.

“I've written the brief that had your fee in it. Here's what was in the other half of the document.”

You're being hired

10 ideas

The moment with the most leverage and the least information. Almost everything here is askable and almost nobody asks.

0.1% sounds like nothing and might be worth more than 1% somewhere else

The usual

Equity gets quoted three ways — percentage, share count, or dollar value at the last round. Which one they lead with tells you what the other two would reveal.

Non-technical roles are typically granted materially less equity than engineers at the same level and stage. It's convention, not policy, which means it's arguable.

Your title is a compensation instrument

The usual

Negotiate the vesting, not the number

The usual

Four years with a one-year cliff is the default. A shortened cliff, monthly vesting from day one, or double-trigger acceleration are all askable and cost the company nothing today.

Travels

The offer letter isn't the document that matters

The usual

The stock plan and the grant agreement carry the terms. They're usually sent after you accept. Ask for both before.

Travels

Ask what the last 409A was

The usual

It's the price your options are struck at and they have the number. Whether they hand it over or get strange is itself the answer.

Travels

“We'll make it right at the next round”

The usual

Worth nothing as a sentence. Worth something as a written number with a date and a trigger attached. Ask for the second version.

Travels

What “competitive” means when a recruiter says it

The usual

Competitive against a benchmark they've chosen and won't name. Ask which dataset, which stage, which geography.

Signing bonus, salary, or equity — which to push on

The usual

Depends on stage. Early: equity, because the rest is small anyway. Late: cash, because the equity is priced and the upside is mostly gone.

The four questions that change how they see you

The usual

Preference stack, fully diluted count, last 409A, exercise window. Asking these reads as sophisticated rather than greedy, which is itself useful information about the company.

02

You already signed something

11 ideas

The paperwork most people read once, at the least sceptical moment of their lives.

Equity, options, RSUs, phantom — sorted by what they do to you

The usual

At a private company pre-IPO, options are most common. RSUs arrive later. Phantom shows up in agencies, family businesses and anything not venture-backed — which is where a lot of brand and creative people actually work.

Travels

Phantom stock is a bonus wearing a costume

The usual

It's a contractual promise to pay cash tied to a valuation. No ownership, no voting, no capital gains treatment, and it dies if the company decides it does. Agencies love it for exactly those reasons.

Check the numberTravels

Your ninety-day window is the most expensive line in the contract

The usual

Ninety days after you leave to exercise, or the options vanish. Some companies offer extended windows of up to ten years. Almost nobody asks which one they're on until they resign.

Travels

Find your cliff date and put it in your calendar

The usual

One year from start. Leaving in month eleven means you worked a year for salary alone. Nobody at the company will ever remind you.

Travels

Dilution is not theft, and here is when it is

The usual

Every round issues new shares and your percentage shrinks. That's normal. A pool refresh taken out of the pre-money, so existing holders absorb all of it, is normal too — and worth knowing you're absorbing.

Liquidation preference in one sentence

The usual

Investors get their money back before anyone else sees a dollar. One-times non-participating is standard. Anything above that appears when a company raised from a position of weakness.

Travels

Participating preferred, plainly

The usual

They take their money back and then also take their percentage of what's left. Double dip. Legal, not rare, and it moves the sale price at which you see anything.

Travels

The non-solicit is the clause that matters more than the non-compete

The usual

In a lot of places non-competes are hard to enforce. Non-solicits are enforced routinely, and they're the ones that stop you taking your team or your clients.

Check the number

You might not be allowed to show your own work

The usual

Work-for-hire assigns everything, including the right to display it. The portfolio carve-out is almost always granted and almost never requested.

Travels

What happens to your equity if you go freelance for the same company

The usual

Usually you stop vesting on the day the employment ends, even if the work continues. Continuity of work is not continuity of grant.

Check the number

The AI clause nobody is reading yet

The usual

Newer contracts contain language about generating derivative or synthetic material from delivered work. Worth finding out whether you've licensed them the right to produce more of you without you.

03

You're setting your price

10 ideas

Where the money is actually won or lost for most people in this audience — repeatedly, quietly, for years.

You're quoting cost-to-serve and calling it a rate

The usual

Three different numbers: what it costs you to do it, what the market pays, what the outcome is worth to them. Most people quote the first and never find out the third.

Travels

What the agency charges the client for the work you do

The usual

Agencies bill a multiple of salary cost to cover overhead, benefits, management and margin. Going freelance at your old day rate means charging a fraction of what the work sells for.

Check the numberTravels

How many billable days you actually have

The usual

Not 260. Subtract holiday, admin, pitching, invoicing, sick days and the weeks nothing lands. Solo practices run well under full utilisation and the annual number is the one that matters.

Check the numberTravels

The day rate is a trap and here's the arithmetic

The usual

Day rates invite counting days, cap your upside at your calendar, and punish you for getting faster. Project fees pay you for being good at this.

Travels

Kill fees exist and you should have one

The usual

A percentage of the remaining fee if they cancel mid-project. Standard in production and editorial, rare in consulting only because consultants don't ask.

Check the numberTravels

Net 60 is an interest-free loan you're extending

The usual

Large companies default to 30, 60 or 90 day terms set by procurement, not by your client. It's negotiable more often at the contract stage than at the invoice stage.

Travels

Quote a range only if you'd be happy with the bottom of it

The usual

They will hear the bottom. It isn't a negotiation tactic, it's a discount you offered before being asked.

Travels

Deposits: 50/50, or 30/40/30

The usual

Money up front is standard practice, not an insult. A deposit is the thing that makes a cancellation expensive for them instead of only for you.

Raising rates on a client you already have

The usual

Tie it to a date and a new scope rather than to your feelings about the work. The re-scope conversation is easier than the raise conversation and produces the same number.

Travels

The qualifying questions before you scope anything

The usual

Budget range, decision-maker, timeline, and what happens if they do nothing. Scoping before you know these is unpaid work.

04

You're doing a deal

13 ideas

Your own ground. The part where you've read the brief from the other side.

You sold a post. They bought an asset.

The usual

Paying for a post buys the post, organic, on your channel. Running it as an ad is buying media against something you made, and it's a separate line with a separate number.

Travels

Search the contract for the word perpetuity

The usual

Term, territory, channel and spend are the four axes that price usage. Perpetuity removes one of them entirely. If it's in there and the fee didn't move, the fee was wrong.

Travels

Whitelisting, explained by what it costs you

The usual

They run paid media through your handle so it reads as you. Your account carries the endorsement and their budget carries the reach. Price it as endorsement, not as a technical permission.

Travels

Exclusivity is forgone revenue, not a favour

The usual

Price it as the deals you won't be able to take in that window. Twelve months of category exclusivity when you book four of those a year is most of your year.

Travels

Which budget is paying you sets your ceiling

The usual

Brand, performance, innovation, trade, or the founder's discretionary line. Same deliverable, four different tolerances, four different approval paths.

Travels

The person emailing you cannot say yes

The usual

Signing authority sits at a threshold, and the threshold is usually lower than people assume. Write the email that survives being forwarded upward, because it will be.

Travels

“We don't have budget” has five meanings

The usual

Not in this pot, not this quarter, not at this priority, no internal champion, or genuinely none. Four of the five are timing problems rather than a no.

Travels

Seeding: on-ramp or unpaid labour

The usual

Seeding is normal and often genuinely useful early. It stops being an on-ramp the moment the brand is running paid media and still sending product instead of a fee.

Travels

EMV is a number brands know is soft

The usual

Earned media value is a modelled figure with no agreed methodology. It appears in decks because something has to. Knowing it's soft changes how you argue about your own reporting.

Check the number

Ask what the client is being charged for you

The usual

When an agency sits between you and the brand, there's a markup. It's normal. Knowing roughly what it is tells you where your rate actually sits in the deal.

Rev share without a floor is a free option

The usual

If you don't control the landing page, the pricing, the stock, or the attribution window, you've taken performance risk on decisions you can't influence.

Travels

Why deals die internally, and how to keep yours alive

The usual

Rarely price. Usually a champion who moved, a quarter that closed, a legal queue, or nobody senior who could describe why you specifically.

What gets you re-booked

The usual

Being easy to run through a legal and finance process. Unglamorous, commercially decisive, and almost never discussed as a skill.

You're reading the room

7 ideas

The part that isn't in any contract and decides most outcomes.

Read the org chart for authority, not title

The usual

Economic buyer, champion, blocker. Titles tell you rank. Only one of those three can actually release money.

Information asymmetry is the real currency

The usual

Everything in this series is known by someone in the room already. The advantage isn't intelligence, it's having been in the room before.

What you're owed when you're paid in access

The usual

Access, proximity, information and optionality are real compensation and should be priced. The tell that it's exposure with extra steps: remove the access and see if you'd still take it.

The usual

Decide it when you're calm, write it down, and don't revise it while someone is talking to you.

The usual

The hardest version, and the one nobody makes content about. What you can still control: silence, sequencing, and what you ask before you answer.

Travels

Small industry, long memory

The usual

How you leave is a commercial asset. The people on the other side of your next three deals already know someone you've worked with.

Sponsorship versus mentorship

The usual

A mentor tells you things. A sponsor says your name when you aren't there. Only one of them changes your income.

Money actually moves

9 ideas

The endings. Most people only picture one of them.

Five endings, not one

The usual

IPO, strategic acquisition, acquihire, private equity roll-up, or private forever. Your grant behaves differently under each and most people have only modelled the first.

Travels

Acquihire: the team is the asset and the product gets switched off

The usual

Common, rarely announced as such, and the outcome where employee equity most often goes to nothing while senior people are made whole through retention packages.

Retention pools are carved out of the purchase price

The usual

Which means the headline number and the amount available to shareholders are different numbers, and being inside the pool matters more than your grant does.

Earnouts: why founders stay two more years looking miserable

The usual

Part of the price is contingent on hitting targets after the sale. It's why the acquisition number in the press release is often not the number anyone receives.

Travels

Secondaries: how people get paid before an IPO

The usual

The company organises a window, existing shares change hands, no new money enters the business. Increasingly the main way private-company employees see cash.

Travels

Sold for two hundred million, team got nothing, nobody did anything wrong

The usual

The preference stack was in the documents from the beginning. This is the single most useful thing in the whole series and it takes forty seconds.

Travels

Borrowing against equity, including the part nobody says

The usual

You can turn illiquid shares into cash by giving someone a claim on them. If the valuation falls the loan doesn't, and the collateral is the thing you were trying to keep.

Travels

What happens to unvested shares in each exit

The usual

Usually they're assumed, converted, or cancelled, and which one is decided by the acquirer, not by you. Double-trigger acceleration is the clause that protects against the third.

Where this still needs you

The fifteen flagged numbers
Every idea marked in pink states a benchmark I have not verified against a current source. Carta and Pave publish equity band data; agency multiples and utilisation rates vary enough by market that a single figure is misleading. Get the real number or reframe the idea so it doesn't need one — saying a wrong benchmark confidently is the one failure this whole series cannot survive.
Your side of the table
Chapter 04 is the only one where you can say “I've written the brief your fee was in.” That sentence is worth more than any statistic here and it can't be borrowed. Mark which of those thirteen you've personally seen go wrong.
Rad School of Growth
Still unreachable from this session — it's a Claude Project and no tool here opens one. Chapters 03 and 06 are the ones your existing frameworks should be carrying, and right now they're carrying mine.
The partnerships session
Same. Chapter 04 is a first pass built from general practice. It's the chapter most likely to be wrong in the specifics and the one you're most qualified to correct.
What's deliberately missing
No tax content, no investment advice, no “here's what to do with the money.” Different liability, different register, and the moment the series gives financial advice it stops being yours and starts being regulated.
