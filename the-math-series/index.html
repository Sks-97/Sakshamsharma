The call

Yes, there's a series here. Not the one you described.

“MBA for hot girls” is a great tone brief and a bad franchise name. Keep the first job, drop the second.

The instruction it encodes — low solemnity, high taste, explain the real thing without the reverence — should govern every single script. But as the name of the thing, it puts you fourth in a line behind people with bigger audiences and finance credentials you don't claim, doing a format that is now the most commoditised thing on the internet: the explainer. Any chatbot explains a 409A. Nobody else can tell you what happened in the room when the 409A mattered.

The version only you can make: you were in the rooms. You've sat on the buying side of a creator deal. You've watched founders sign things. The series isn't “finance for women” — it's how money and power actually move through the creative economy, from someone who has been on both sides of the table and will say which side she was on.

06

A valuation is not a price

It's the number one investor paid for one slice on one day, multiplied out across every share as if they'd buy them all at that price. They would not. Post-money valuation as a mostly-fictional artifact everyone agrees to treat as real.

Anchor
Canva's $42B is what the tender was priced at. A single buyer setting a clearing price for a sliver is not a market valuing the whole company.
Pundit
07

Secondaries: how paper becomes money before an IPO

The mechanic in full. A tender offer is the company organising a sanctioned window where employees sell existing shares to incoming investors. No new money enters the company. This is the day the original podcast question actually gets answered.

Anchor
Canva, Aug 2025: $1,646.14/share, cap of $3m per employee, buyers included Fidelity and JPMorgan's asset management arm, and it was reported significantly oversubscribed.

08

Why a company lets you cash out (it isn't generosity)

Tenders are a retention instrument. Staff who've been there eight years with no liquidity leave. A tender resets the clock without an IPO. And “oversubscribed” tells you something specific: more people wanted in than were let in, which is a price signal the company chose to publish.

Anchor
Companies rarely run a tender immediately before listing — which is why Canva's read as a signal the IPO was still some distance out.

Borrowing against equity — the house thing

The part of the podcast that sounded like a cheat code. You can borrow against private shares to buy a house. What that actually is, who lends, and the thing nobody says out loud: if the valuation falls, the loan doesn't, and the collateral is the shares.

Anchor
Frame as the trade it is: you're converting an illiquid asset into liquid cash by giving someone a claim on the asset. Name the downside case explicitly.

Day 05

The line that decides whether you get anything

~52s · pundit

There's a line in almost every investment document that decides whether you get anything, and almost nobody reading an offer letter has read it. Liquidation preference. Investors get their money back before anyone else sees a dollar.

So — company raises thirty million over its life, sells for thirty-five, which by any normal reading is a good outcome. You sold a company. Investors take thirty. Five is left for everyone who worked there. And if the preference is participating, or two-x — which happens when you raise in a bad market — investors take sixty before anybody else gets anything, and there is no sale price at which you get paid.

So when you read that a company sold for two hundred million and the team got nothing, nobody did anything wrong. It was in the document the whole time.

Ask what the preference stack is before you take the job. They'll tell you. Nobody ever asks.

Day 13

Usage rights are the most underpriced line in the contract

~46s · pundit

Can we talk about usage rights, because it's the most underpriced line in every creator contract and I've been on the side of the table that benefits from that.

When a brand pays for a post, they're paying for the post. Organic, your channel, your people. The moment they run it as an ad, or put it on their page, or whitelist it through your handle so it looks like you but they're paying to push it — that's a different product. You made the asset, they're buying media against it.

And the number isn't “plus twenty percent,” it's tied to term, territory and spend. Six weeks of paid social in one market is not perpetuity, all channels, worldwide — which, I promise you, is sitting in a lot of these contracts.

Look for the word perpetuity. If it's in there and your rate didn't move, you sold the whole thing for the price of a post.

Production

Six shoot days, not thirty

Batch by module

Same discipline as your macro-trends series: one outfit, one setup, one sitting per module. Five scripts per shoot. The weekly visual change becomes the series' structure — viewers learn that a new look means a new subject.

Write in blocks of five

Scripts for a module get written together so the arc within the week actually holds. Week one has to end on day five landing hardest — that's the one that gets sent to a group chat.

Keep one day loose per week

The series runs six weeks. Something will happen in the news that belongs in it. Hold day 4 of each module as swappable rather than pretending the plan survives contact.
